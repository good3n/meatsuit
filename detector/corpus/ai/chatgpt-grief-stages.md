Grief is a deeply personal response to loss. It can follow the death of someone you love, but it can also emerge after a breakup, divorce, serious illness, job loss, miscarriage, major life transition, or any experience that changes the future you thought you were going to have.
Although grief is universal, the way it feels can vary dramatically from one person to another. Some people experience intense sadness right away. Others initially feel numb, angry, confused, or even strangely calm. Grief can affect your mood, sleep, appetite, concentration, relationships, energy level, and physical health.
One of the most familiar ways of describing grief is through the five stages of grief: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance. These stages can provide language for some of the emotions people experience after loss, but they are not a checklist or a roadmap everyone follows.
Understanding the stages of grief can help you recognize that many difficult reactions to loss are normal. More importantly, it can help you approach grief with less pressure to feel a certain way or recover according to a particular timeline.
What is Grief?
Grief is the emotional, psychological, and physical response that can occur after experiencing a meaningful loss.
People often associate grief with death, but bereavement is only one form of loss. You may also grieve the end of a relationship, the loss of a friendship, changes in your health, infertility, moving away from a familiar community, losing a job, or watching an important part of your identity change.
Grief may involve sadness, but sadness is only one possible reaction. You might also experience:

* Anger or irritability
* Guilt or regret
* Anxiety
* Numbness
* Loneliness
* Confusion
* Difficulty concentrating
* Changes in sleep
* Changes in appetite
* Fatigue
* Physical tension or discomfort
* A sense that life feels unfamiliar or unreal

Some people also experience moments of relief, happiness, humor, or peace while grieving. Those moments do not mean you cared any less about what you lost.
Grief can be unpredictable. You may feel relatively okay one day and overwhelmed the next. A song, photograph, anniversary, location, smell, conversation, or unexpected memory can suddenly bring strong emotions back.
There is no single correct way to grieve.
What are the stages of grief?
The five stages of grief are commonly described as denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance.
The framework is associated with psychiatrist Elisabeth Kübler-Ross, who introduced the stages in her 1969 book On Death and Dying. Importantly, the original model was developed through Kübler-Ross's work with people facing terminal illness. Over time, the stages became widely used as a way of discussing bereavement and other forms of loss.
Today, many mental health professionals view the stages as possible emotional experiences rather than a strict sequence everyone goes through.
1. Denial
Denial can be the mind's way of absorbing something that feels too overwhelming to fully process at once.
After a loss, you might find yourself thinking:
“This can't be happening.”
“I keep expecting them to walk through the door.”
“There must have been a mistake.”
You may understand intellectually that the loss occurred while still struggling to emotionally accept that your life has changed.
Denial does not necessarily mean literally believing the event did not happen. It can appear as numbness, disbelief, avoidance, emotional detachment, or difficulty talking about the loss.
For some people, this temporary emotional distance creates space to begin processing a painful reality gradually rather than all at once.
2. Anger
As the reality of a loss begins to sink in, anger can become more noticeable.
You may feel angry at the person who died, doctors, family members, yourself, a former partner, an employer, your circumstances, or even life in general.
You might think:
“Why did this happen?”
“This isn't fair.”
“Someone should have prevented this.”
Anger may also hide other painful emotions underneath it, including fear, helplessness, abandonment, or sadness.
People sometimes feel guilty for being angry while grieving, especially when the anger is directed toward someone they love. But emotions do not always follow logic. Experiencing anger does not mean you are cruel or ungrateful. It may simply be one way your mind is trying to make sense of something painful.
3. Bargaining
Bargaining often involves mentally revisiting what happened and imagining ways the outcome might have been different.
It can sound like:
“If only I had called sooner.”
“What if we had gone to another doctor?”
“If I had noticed the signs, maybe things would be different.”
After some losses, bargaining may also involve promises or attempts to regain a sense of control.
Someone might think, “If I change everything about my life, maybe things will somehow be okay again.”
Bargaining can be closely connected to guilt because people often focus on decisions they made before the loss. In hindsight, events can appear much clearer than they actually were at the time.
Working through grief sometimes involves recognizing the difference between genuine responsibility and the natural human tendency to search for explanations when something painful happens.
4. Depression
The depression stage refers to the deep sadness that can appear as the reality of a loss becomes harder to avoid.
A person may experience:

* Frequent crying
* Low motivation
* Withdrawal from other people
* Difficulty enjoying activities
* Changes in sleep
* Changes in appetite
* Exhaustion
* Feelings of emptiness
* Difficulty imagining the future

Feeling depressed during grief does not automatically mean someone has clinical depression. Sadness, withdrawal, fatigue, and changes in daily functioning can be natural reactions to significant loss.
However, grief and mental health conditions can overlap. If emotional pain becomes overwhelming, lasts for an extended period without improvement, severely interferes with daily functioning, or includes thoughts of self-harm or suicide, professional support is especially important.
5. Acceptance
Acceptance does not mean being happy about what happened.
It also does not mean forgetting someone, approving of a loss, or reaching a point where the experience never hurts again.
Acceptance means gradually recognizing the reality of what happened and learning how to continue living alongside it.
You may begin thinking about the future again. Everyday routines may become easier. Memories might bring warmth along with sadness instead of only pain.
Acceptance can also involve creating a new relationship with what was lost.
For someone grieving a death, that might mean finding meaningful ways to remember the person while continuing with life. After a breakup, acceptance might mean recognizing that the relationship mattered while also beginning to imagine a future without it.
Grief may still return even after acceptance. The difference is that those emotions may gradually become more manageable.
Are there 5 or 7 stages of grief?
You may encounter both five-stage and seven-stage models of grief.
The best-known framework is the five-stage Kübler-Ross model:

1. Denial
2. Anger
3. Bargaining
4. Depression
5. Acceptance

Other versions expand the process into seven stages. The exact names vary depending on the source, but a seven-stage model may include experiences such as shock, denial, pain, guilt, anger, bargaining, depression, reconstruction, or acceptance.
Neither model should be viewed as a scientifically fixed sequence that every grieving person must complete.
Grief research has increasingly emphasized that people respond to loss in highly individual ways. Some people recognize themselves in several stages. Others experience only one or two. Some experience emotions that are not represented by the model at all.
For that reason, the stages may be most useful as a vocabulary for grief rather than instructions for how grief is supposed to happen.
Instead of asking, “Which stage should I be in?” it may be more helpful to ask, “What am I experiencing right now, and what kind of support do I need?”
How long do the stages of grief last — and do they happen in order?
There is no standard amount of time for each stage of grief.
A stage could last hours, days, weeks, or longer. You may experience several emotions during the same day or feel as though you have returned to an earlier stage months after you thought you had moved past it.
Most importantly, the stages do not have to happen in order.
You might feel angry before experiencing denial. You may reach a sense of acceptance and then suddenly experience intense sadness around an anniversary. You may feel relatively peaceful for several weeks before something unexpectedly brings anger or grief back to the surface.
Grief often looks less like climbing a staircase and more like moving through waves.
Over time, those waves may become less frequent or less overwhelming, but reminders of a meaningful loss can continue for years.
There is also no universal deadline for “getting over” grief. The goal is generally not to erase the loss but to gradually adapt to life after it.
However, some people experience grief that remains extremely intense and disruptive for a prolonged period. When grief consistently prevents someone from functioning, maintaining relationships, caring for themselves, or participating in everyday life, additional professional support may be helpful.
Therapy for grief and loss
You do not have to wait until grief becomes unbearable before talking with a therapist.
Grief therapy can provide a private space to process complicated emotions, talk openly about the person or situation you lost, and develop strategies for coping with difficult moments.
A therapist may help you work through emotions such as guilt, anger, sadness, fear, or resentment without judging those reactions.
Therapy may also be particularly helpful when grief is connected to traumatic circumstances, unresolved relationship issues, sudden death, suicide, complicated family dynamics, or multiple losses happening close together.
Different therapeutic approaches may be used depending on your experience and needs. For example, cognitive behavioral therapy may help address unhelpful thought patterns connected to guilt or self-blame. Trauma-focused therapy may be useful when a loss involved disturbing or frightening circumstances. Support groups can also provide a sense of connection with people who understand similar experiences.
Therapy does not attempt to make you forget someone or stop caring about what happened.
Instead, the goal is often to help you integrate the loss into your life in a way that allows you to continue moving forward.
Support can also come from outside therapy. Friends, family members, faith communities, grief support groups, journaling, exercise, creative activities, and maintaining everyday routines can all play a role in coping.
The most helpful approach is often the one that allows you to experience your emotions without letting grief completely isolate you from the rest of your life.
Takeaway
The five stages of grief — denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance — can help describe some of the emotions people experience after a major loss.
But grief rarely follows a predictable sequence.
You may experience every stage, only a few of them, or emotions that do not fit neatly into any stage at all. You may move forward, backward, or between several emotions at once. There is also no universal timeline for how long grief should last.
Rather than measuring your grief against a particular model, try to view the stages as possible experiences rather than requirements.
Loss can permanently change parts of your life, but the intensity of grief often changes with time. Healing does not require forgetting what happened or leaving someone behind. It means gradually finding a way to carry the loss while continuing to live, connect with others, and create meaningful experiences.
If grief feels overwhelming or is making it difficult to function in everyday life, talking with a mental health professional can provide additional support as you work through the loss.
